## Abstract

Implementing statistical and learning models in industrial contexts is a promising but challenging objective.
Indeed, the setup and guarantees that an application requires often differ from those that the method assumes and provides.
An example arises when monitoring a system at an evolving operating point such as its **state**.
Hypothesis tests are the standard method to detect unexpected behaviors, yet there is no general way of including such operating points in tests.
Another example involves classifying a system (for instance, as faulty or healthy) from past measurements.
Yet, they are only a proxy for the object to be classified (the system itself), contrary to what learning algorithms expect.
Such issues systematically arise in the context of **dynamical systems**, which are phenomena that evolve over time according to a fixed rule, which is only accessed through observations.
The usual solution involves reformulating the objective into a technical question amenable to the method.
The goal of this thesis is to facilitate this step with hypothesis tests and learning algorithms that accommodate the assumptions of dynamical systems.

The first line of research involves **local hypothesis tests**, which consider jointly potentially infinite collections of hypotheses indexed by a **covariate**.
Local tests are immediately relevant to dynamical systems; e.g., states can be covariates, and statements involving dynamics evaluated at states are local hypotheses.
I establish the connection between local tests and learning theory.
This yields a general method for constructing local tests, together with an instatiation based on kernel methods.

The second line of research concerns **learning on surrogate inputs**: learning when inputs are not directly measured, but only estimated.
Such methods operate on the level of dynamical systems directly, representing them for instance as probability distributions.
I provide the first evaluation of such an algorithm on real data and extend its theoretical analysis using tools from statistical learning.

Together, these contributions expand the toolkit of statistical methods available for dynamical systems.
